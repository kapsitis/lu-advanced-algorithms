Given that GF(2^8) has 256, or 16^2 elements (including 0), it's well-suited for a square 2D visualization. Imagine a 16x16 grid where each cell in the grid represents an element of the field.

A visual way to show the generation of the field via a primitive polynomial could be to color-code the cells based on the power of the generator that produces them.

Begin with the generator and light up the first cell. Then, multiply the generator by itself and light up the appropriate cell that corresponds to the product. Continue this process. The rule of multiplication by the generator will provide a unique path through the grid, eventually hitting every non-zero element. In fact, it will hit all of them in a certain order — defined by their logarithms base "generator".

In other words, the picture will show a sort of a cycle, with 255 steps, around the elements of the non-zero field.

This can be a powerful way to show how the entire non-zero field is generated by a single element, and can provide some intuition for why the non-zero elements of a finite field always form a group under multiplication.

Inverting elements can be shown as moving an object in the opposite direction along the cycle. Basically, element "x" transforms into "x^{254}". Multiplication of "x" by "x" moves it one step forward; 254 multiplications will make almost the whole round and return us back to "x"; only we will find that the direction of transformation was "backwards".

Each element could be color-coded by its logarithm base "generator" - to show a path along the non-zero elements.

Finally, operations could be animated: addition, subtraction, multiplication, divide, calculate "inverse" - all these might be shown as transformations on this 16x16 grid - by showing the transformation of a "point" element, or of a whole "image". 

This will not bring the whole understanding, but at least some geometric interpretation of GF(2^8).
